# Voice

This file tells the skill how you sound so edits keep sounding like you. Replace the example below with your own voice. Anything here overrides the skill's general rules when they conflict. Delete the file if you want the general rules only.

Describe your voice in four parts:

1. **How I sound.** Cadence, tone, sentence length, how blunt or hedged you are. If you write in more than one register (chat vs. docs), describe each so editing doesn't pull one toward the other.
2. **Formats I write in.** The kinds of pieces you write and how each should read.
3. **My conventions.** House rules the editor should enforce, not remove.
4. **Words.** Words you actually use that should survive editing, and words you never want.

Tip: mine your own past writing for traits, and quote real lines as evidence. The traits you'd never think to name (how you handle numbers, how you push back, your connector punctuation) are usually the most distinctive.

## Example (a product manager's voice)

**How I sound.** Two registers. In chat: lowercase, no closing periods, one thought per message sent in bursts, casual interjections ("woah," "no prob") and abbreviations (w/, est, wk) stay. In channel posts and docs: proper capitalization, still punchy, short paragraphs. I state the number, then the takeaway. I'd rather be specific and wrong than vague and safe. Deliberate fragments and colon-led punch lines are part of my voice, not slop.

**Formats I write in.** Exec updates lead with the delta since last time, never a recap. Slack messages get to the ask in the first two lines, name an owner, and cc the stakeholders. Retros are blame-free: name the mechanism that failed, not the person. Long-form docs use a fixed shape: a TLDR paragraph leads (problem, then support, then the thesis), every body paragraph opens with a topic sentence the rest supports, and a conclusion wraps it back together. That wrap-up conclusion is deliberate structure, not a summary-recap ending to cut.

**My conventions.**
- Unpack judgments. State the fact or gap first, then the tradeoff in plain terms: "While trace-id can close the gap, it has these setbacks." Never fold the verdict into a clever phrase like "trace-id is the expensive way to close it."
- Show the math inline, not just the conclusion: "Baseline: 4.1% of 2.2M = 90K. Actual: 310K."
- Flag the artifact in my own good news instead of smoothing it into a clean win: "310K came back. Not so fast: most returned before the send." Default to the conservative baseline and say so.
- Acknowledge, then answer: "you make a good point. Our goal is X, so either path works."
- Caveats go in an appendix bullet at the end, not woven through the narrative.
- Bold the one number or decision that matters in an update. Nothing else.
- Spaced hyphens are my connector ("yeah - agreed, conservative is better"). Keep them. No em dashes, ever.
- Light emoji in chat is fine, never in docs or headings.

**Words.** "Leverage," "streamline," and "robust" are normal vocabulary in my world; keep them when they're doing work. Never: cutting-edge, game changer, this is huge.
